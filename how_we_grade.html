How We Grade

Every grade on The Vault can be traced back to numbers you can check yourself. This page explains where they come from and exactly how each verdict is decided. The figures shown are read live from the site's own settings, so they're always current.

Where the numbers come from

  • Player and pick values are KeepTradeCut's dynasty values, refreshed every night, in your league's format (superflex or 1QB, and your tight end premium).
  • Your league — rosters, records, schedule, scoring, and roster slots — comes straight from Sleeper.
  • Projected points are weekly projections scored with your league's actual scoring settings, not a generic PPR number.
  • Value history is a daily record of every value going back to 2020, which is what lets us price a past trade on the day it happened.

Good trade or bad trade?

The Trade Calculator leads with one answer for your team, on a ladder: Great trade, Good trade, Slight edge, Toss-up, Leans bad, Bad trade or Terrible trade, with one sentence saying why and one line with that tier's real track record for your timeline. It weighs what you'd gain or give up in value, how the trade fits your roster and your timeline, and, if you're playing to win now, what it does to your lineup this season. Contending, this season counts heavily (2 points of score for every point a week your lineup gains or loses, plus half the value-and-fit read); rebuilding, it's value and how the trade fits your roster (not the timeline fit: rewarding picks and getting younger made rebuilders' verdicts less accurate on real trades, since managers overpay for picks at the draft); a flexible timeline sits in between. Your timeline can be switched right next to the verdict, because the same trade can be good for a rebuilder and bad for a contender.

Example: Ja'Marr Chase and Joe Burrow for Patrick Mahomes, George Pickens and three 1sts. You'd get about 10% more value, but as the league favorite it costs you 3.9 points a week and about 5 points of title odds: on the losing side contending, on the winning side rebuilding. The price grade below (Fair, Lopsided, Unfair) still says whether the price is fair for both teams, but it's supporting detail now. Trade Coach ranks its suggestions by the same score and only lists trades rated Slight edge or better (Sell high, Buy low and Shop a player also show Toss-ups, since those are about price), at prices managers actually pay for every player in them.

Where the tiers come from. Real trades in completed 2025 and 2024 Sleeper dynasty leagues (about 30,000, from 2,800 leagues), each judged as its league stood that day with that week's projections, then checked against what happened. A score just past even turned out to be a coin flip, so the tiers sit where real outcomes actually separate: +25 and up is a Great trade (88% of contenders were still ahead on value a year later, on trade value), +15 a Good trade, +10 a Slight edge, −6 to +10 a Toss-up (about half came out ahead), then Leans bad, Bad trade (below −15) and Terrible trade (below −25; 9% of contenders still ahead). Each tier came out in the same order on the half of the leagues held back to check it, and in every league format. The line under the verdict quotes your tier's record for your timeline: the share still ahead on value a year later. The lineup math's predicted points showed up at 91% with real weekly projections. Two things were tested and left out because they didn't help: knocking contenders for buying older players (contenders who did that came out ahead more often, since sellers price vets to move) and for a Superflex team selling its 3rd QB (the lineup and depth math already catch it).

Tested on real trades (the first test, of the earlier three answers: Good trade at +3, Bad trade at −3). Every two-team trade in 2,924 completed 2025 Sleeper dynasty leagues (27,327 trades), each judged as its league stood that day and checked against what happened next. In-season, the side the verdict called a Good trade scored 5.7 more points a week than its partner afterward, and the Bad trade side 6.3 fewer; the lineup math's predicted change showed up at 79%. Contenders with a Good trade made the playoffs 86% of the time against 82% expected, and with a Bad trade 66% against 68%. Sides called Good were still ahead on value a year later 69% of the time. What it doesn't do is predict acceptance: managers accept trades it calls Bad for them all the time (only 18% of real trades left both teams not worse), so whether a trade is likely to happen comes from the market's price ranges instead.

Fair, Lopsided, Unfair

A trade's gap is measured in % off: how far apart the two sides are, relative to the average side, on trade value. Trade value uses KeepTradeCut's player and pick values, combined the way managers actually combine them: one great player is worth more than several decent ones that add up to the same raw value, because roster spots and starting slots are limited. The best piece on a side counts in full and each extra piece for less, smoothly. How much less depends on the trade's best piece: in a trade built around a 2,500 player, a second 2,000 piece adds little, because managers rarely take two depth pieces for one starter; in a trade built around an 8,000 star, extra pieces count for more, because real deals for stars usually take two good ones. Like KTC, it's shown as a consolidation premium added to the side with the bigger pieces.

Why not KTC's own adjustment? We graded 1,519,022 completed Sleeper dynasty trades (2021 to 2026, each priced at KTC on its own day, in its own league's format), every one accepted by both managers. On KTC's adjustment only 30% of them grade Fair, and the team getting fewer, better pieces looks like it overpaid by about 27%: in a typical real 1-for-2, the two pieces add up to 1.5 times the one. KTC's bonus fades whenever the best piece coming back is close to the star and jumps around with small changes (a 5,942 player for 5,332 + 3,417 gets +657; 5,189 for 3,308 + 3,159 gets +3,356). Trade value was fitted on the trades before June 2026, so that a typical accepted trade at each value range comes out even, and checked on the 275,293 since: 66% of them grade Fair, and the lean drops to about 1% (from 1-for-2 to 3-for-4 and at every value range from 2,000 up, within 3%; trades of 1 for 4 or more still lean against the consolidator, since managers do accept a pile of pieces for one player at a discount). KTC's own number is still shown next to every grade ("KTC's calculator") so you can check it on KeepTradeCut.

How big the star bonus is. The curve above is fitted so a typical accepted trade comes out even, but what managers accept isn't the same as what wins. So the site uses it at 1.5 times that strength: across 7,460 real 2025 trades with uneven piece counts, that size of bonus picked the teams that went on to score more and beat their playoff and title odds better than the accepted-trade curve did, and far better than KTC's own adjustment (which picked them worse than a coin flip). A bigger bonus still mostly double-counted this season's lineup, which the verdict weighs separately, and did worse at picking next season's winners. Selling a star for two good players costs a little more than it did (Ja'Marr Chase for CeeDee Lamb and Davante Adams: about 8% under, from 2%), and the Fair/Lopsided cutoffs and acceptable ranges hardly move.

Is the market right to pay that premium? Mostly. A year after 118,000 uneven trades, stars hadn't held their value better than the packages paid for them (each side kept about 93-96%), and on trade value the consolidator was ahead 45% of the time. Consolidating into a 7,000+ player held up best (ahead 50% of the time); into a player under 3,000 it did worst (37%); and paying 2.2 times or more in raw KTC value lost 86% of the time. The Trade Calculator shows how teams in the same spot did when one team consolidates.

  • Fair: under off
  • Lopsided: to off
  • Unfair: or more off

Fair is not the same as acceptable. Managers accept unfair trades all the time; that doesn't make them fair. So next to the grade, the Trade Calculator and Trade Grades check the trade against what managers actually pay. Every player and pick has a range: in completed Sleeper trades over the last 45 days where he was the main piece, what the team getting him paid against his KTC value, low to high. Acceptable: some price inside the middle half of every piece's range makes the trade even. A stretch: only the outer range does (the 10th to 90th percentile of what's been paid). Rarely accepted: not even that. Pieces without enough trades of their own get a typical player's range (10% either way, 20% outer). Tested on 9,000 trades the ranges never saw: 81% fit the middle ranges and 97% the outer ones. On the Overall bar, bright white markers show the acceptable range and faint ones the outer range; they lean toward the side the market prices higher. A Lopsided trade can still be acceptable; it never changes the grade.

Those cutoffs were calibrated against about 25,000 completed dynasty trades from KeepTradeCut's trade database, so "Lopsided" means lopsided compared to what real leagues actually accept.

Re-checked on 1 October 2026 against about 137,000 completed trades from Sleeper dynasty leagues, each priced on its own day: the Fair line holds (27.8% of Superflex trades and 24.4% of 1QB trades read Fair). Fewer of these trades read Unfair than in KTC's database (24% of Superflex, 28% of 1QB, against 30%), so if anything the Unfair line is generous.

A Fair trade can be called out further: Great when it's also a strong fit for both teams, and Good when it's a strong fit for one and not a poor fit for the other. A trade that suits one team and hurts the other stays plain Fair, and the Trade Calculator says who it's a poor fit for. Fit can only add those labels to a trade that's already Fair.

The ± next to some grades is how much the less-established pieces (rookies, far-off picks) have swung in price over the last days — a reminder of how settled the number is.

Roster fit and timeline

The overall grade weighs three things. The three small bars under the main bar on the Trade Calculator and Trade Grades show each one.

  • KTC value () — the % off above.
  • Roster fit () — whether the trade fills a real positional need or opens one up, plus how much it moves each team's best possible starting lineup (projected points and VORP).
  • Timeline () — whether the ages and draft picks moving fit each team's plan (rebuilding teams should get younger and add picks; contenders can spend them), and whether it opens, widens, or closes a team's contention window.

The one-way rule: roster fit and timeline can make a trade's grade worse than trade value alone, but never better. If trade value says a trade is lopsided, it reads lopsided here too — fit never talks a real value gap away. Each factor is also capped at so one extreme reading can't swamp the others. When fit does move the grade, both numbers are shown (for example "16% off · KTC 14%").

Grading past trades

Completed trades on Trade Grades are priced at each piece's value on the day the trade happened, not today's. A deal that was fair when it was made shouldn't read as unfair because a player broke out months later. Today's values are shown alongside ("3,180 → 2,678") so you can see how it's aged, and the Managers page shows each manager's net both ways: Net Then and Net Now.

Roster fit and timeline for past trades are judged against each team's current roster and standing.

VORP

Value Over Replacement Player is a player's projected points per game minus what you could get from the best player at the same position who wouldn't start for any team in your league. It makes positions comparable: a quarterback always scores more raw points, but VORP measures the edge over the alternative.

Replacement level is found by filling every team's starting lineup across your league — dedicated slots first, then each flex and superflex going to the best eligible player left — and taking the next player after the last starter at each position.

Playoff odds and contention windows

Projected record, playoff odds, and title odds come from simulating the rest of the season 2,000 times against your league's real remaining schedule, starting from everyone's actual record. Each week is played at that week's strength: the best lineup from who Sleeper projects to play that week, at that week's matchups, blended with the points the team has actually scored per game this season, both compared to the league average. The playoff bracket uses the playoff weeks' lineups. Actual scoring counts more as the season goes on: weeks played ÷ (weeks played + 4), so a third after 2 weeks, half after 4, and two thirds after 8. Games vary about ±22% week to week. Median games count if your league plays them, and the week in progress is included until it's final.

A trade's effect this season (Trade Calculator and Trade Grades) runs the same simulation twice, 4,000 times each, on identical simulated games: once as things stand, and once with each team's strength moved, week by week, by what the trade does to that week's best lineup. Because the games are the same both times, the difference in playoff and title odds comes from the trade, not from luck in the simulation. On Trade Grades, "without" means the same team today with the trade undone; it's skipped when a player from the trade has since moved on.

Injuries and byes. A player's projected points per game is what he scores in the games he plays, from Sleeper's projections for the rest of the season, so it follows his current role rather than what was projected for weeks already played. Projections refresh every 3 hours. During the season, this season's lineup is built week by week for every week left, playoffs included, from the players Sleeper projects to play that week, each adjusted for that week's matchup (his projection that week against his average, kept within 0.6 to 1.5 times), then averaged. Byes count for real, and so do known injuries: a quarterback out for the season after knee surgery adds nothing to whoever trades for him, and a receiver back in week 8 counts from week 8. The Trade Calculator tags these players ("Out for the season (knee)", "Out, back week 8") and shows how many weeks each new starter would start. A player on IR whom Sleeper doesn't project for any week counts as out for the weeks left. Dynasty value is unchanged: KTC prices the injury in.

Depth is what your lineup loses when a starter misses a game. For each starter, the site takes them out, refills your best lineup from the bench using your league's real slots (flex and superflex included, so a spare WR can cover an RB's bye if a flex can hold him), and measures the drop. Each starter is counted as out about 3.5 weeks a season, bye included. That's measured, not guessed: from 2018 to 2025, fantasy starters missed 2.4 games on average plus their bye, and it was nearly the same at every position. During the season the bye and known injuries are already in the week-by-week lineup, so depth covers the injuries nobody knows about yet: 2.5 games per starter. Because about 1 in 6 starters misses 6+ games, depth counts extra as insurance. A trade that raises that cost is marked down in roster fit, with a point of depth counting double a point of healthy-lineup gain, fully for contenders, 60% for flexible teams, and 30% for rebuilders. The This season odds use it at face value.

Offer on the Trade Calculator reads the trade from the other manager's side: trade value plus only their own lineup, needs, and timeline. Managers don't need to come out ahead: across about 24,000 completed dynasty trades in KTC's trade database, the typical one-for-one was about 13% off on KTC value. So a manager is expected to accept being up to about 12% behind, or whatever their own trades in your league show (a manager who never comes out behind stays strict; one who has taken lopsided deals is looser). Getting the best single player in a deal for more pieces counts in their favor, since managers pay about 1.5× KTC value to consolidate. A trade that grades badly for them counts against it, and so does asking for a star straight up: in KTC's trade database only about 1 in 100 trades with an 8,000+ player was one-for-one (about 1 in 20 at 6,000 to 8,000). For a no, their counter is the smallest change they'd accept that still grades Fair for you. Trade Coach uses the same read to pick trades they'd likely take. None of it changes the grade.

A team's contention window is how many of the next five years it projects as a real contender. This season counts when its playoff odds are 50% or better. Later years count when the roster's projected value (with players aging and picks arriving) stays near its peak and its lineup projects at or above the playoff line.

The market

KTC values are what the site grades with. The market is the other half: what managers actually pay in completed dynasty trades, so you can see where a player trades above or below his KTC value. The grade itself never changes because of it.

The market under the grade shows what managers actually paid for the trade's main player, in your league's format (1QB or Superflex), from two sources that never overlap: KTC's trade database for its last few days (priced at today's values), and before that, completed trades from Sleeper dynasty leagues that The Vault collects around the clock, up to 30,000 spread evenly over the rest of the last 45 days (each priced at KTC values from its own day, in its own league's format). Only trades where every piece has a price count, all with the same consolidation math as the grade. It uses trades from leagues as much like yours as it can: your exact settings (team count, Superflex or 1QB, PPR, TE premium) when there are at least 10 such trades, then any team count, then just your QB format and TE premium, then your QB format alone. The line says which it used. The Trade Database's market charts step down the same way (they need about 1,500 trades). It's always the loaded league's own settings, so it works the same for any league.

Market value (player cards, the Player Market page, and "At market prices" in the Trade Calculator) is what managers actually pay for a player: in every completed trade he headlined (from KTC's trade database and the Sleeper dynasty trades The Vault collects, in leagues as much like yours as there are enough of), what the team getting him sent compared with what his side was worth, both on trade value (so the normal premium for getting the better single piece is already counted, and a star isn't read as overpriced just for being one piece), as a median, applied to his KTC value today. Every trade shape counts: across all of them each KTC value level comes out about even with KTC, so the median isn't tilted toward stars or cheap players. (Like-for-like trades alone read every star low, since a star's partner can only be worth less, and consolidation trades alone read cheap players high. Getting a star for more pieces costs only about 6% beyond KTC's own consolidation bonus.) It needs at least 5 such trades (10 to narrow to leagues closer to yours). Market highs & lows on the Player Market page compare each player with others at the same position and KTC value instead, so a whole position trading under KTC (tight ends do, against KTC's TE-premium prices) doesn't fill the list.

Over or under similar players. Next to a market price, "12% over" means he goes for 12% more than players at his position and KTC value (within about 15%) go for, not 12% over his KTC value. That's because KTC squeezes its low end: under about 2,000 nearly every player goes for more than KTC (the median is about 20% over, since the cheapest piece you can send back is usually worth more), so "over KTC" said little about the player himself. Measured against players like him, cheap players split over and under like everyone else. The price itself is still what he actually goes for, and At market prices and Trade Coach's edges use it. Your market, Sell high, Buy low, the Market column on Player Rankings and Rising & cooling all rank this way (Rising & cooling by how big a move is for players at that value, since cheap players' prices swing more).

At market prices (under the grade on the Trade Calculator) prices each player 60% of the way from his KTC value to his market value (picks, and players without enough trades, stay at KTC): replayed day by day on about 500,000 completed trades, about 60% of a player's recent premium carried over to his next trades, and pricing at 60% of it predicted them best. The trade-value consolidation premium is kept exactly as the grade computes it; only each side's players are repriced. Trade Coach's Best trades ranks by the grade plus this market edge, so trades that are even on KTC but sell what goes for more and buy what goes for less come first, and says which player makes the difference. The grade itself never uses market prices.

The trades behind it (under a player's chart on Player Market) lists the completed trades his market value comes from, newest first, so you can check the number yourself. The most extreme tenth at each end (lopsided or partly recorded trades) is faded, since a median barely moves for them.

Your market (top of Player Market) shows which of your players and picks go for more or less than similar players, and which moved most this week. Sell high and Buy low in Trade Coach act on it: sell what goes for more than players like it, for more than its KTC value, and buy what goes for less, for less.

Who pays up (Managers) shows what each manager in your league has paid over or under market, by the position of the best piece they got. Sell high shows the managers who pay up for a position first.

Rookie drafts (Player Market) shows where each rookie actually went in Sleeper dynasty rookie drafts in your QB format, and what each pick slot bought: KTC's value for the pick on draft day next to the draft-day KTC value of the players taken there.

How often he changes hands (player card, Player Market lookup) counts the completed trades a player was part of in the last 30 days, next to the 10 players at his position closest to his KTC value. It's how often, not how easily: stars change hands less than the players just below them because their owners rarely sell, not because nobody's buying. Picks are left out, since they're tracked by year and round with every tier together.

Does the market predict KTC? We checked: across about 35,000 weekly reads from 2022 to 2026, players managers were paying well above KTC for didn't go on to gain more KTC value over the next 30 or 60 days than anyone else (about half rose more than the market either way, in every band). So "the market" here is what you can get in a trade now, not a forecast of where KTC is headed.

Manager styles

The trading styles on the Managers page come from simple rules you can check against a manager's own trades:

  • Consolidator — sends more pieces than they get back in 60%+ of deals. Depth builder — the reverse.
  • Youth & picks buyer — gets younger by half a year or more per deal on average, or nets picks in most deals. Win-now buyer — the reverse.
  • Value trader — no clear lean either way.
  • Active or Occasional is added when a manager trades 1.5× or ½× the league average.

Managers are ranked by Net Now: the value of all their trades at today's KTC prices. Net Then is the same trades at the prices on each trade's day, and the difference is how much their trades gained or lost afterward. A manager whose deals looked bad on the day but have aged well ranks higher, because buying before the market moves is the skill. Best and worst move are judged the same way.

Player stats on Compare Players

Weekly stats for every QB, RB, WR, and TE since 2018 come from Sleeper's box scores (charted by Sportradar). Every week is scored with your league's settings, so fantasy points, weekly finishes, "top-12 weeks," and "starter weeks" are all in your scoring — a starter week means finishing inside the number of players at that position your league actually starts.

The # next to a stat is its rank among every player at the same position over the exact games you picked. To be ranked, a player needs at least 40% as many of those games as the busiest player at the position, and rate stats (catch rate, yards per carry, …) also need at least a quarter of the top volume — so a two-catch game can't top the catch-rate list. The shading runs green (best) to red (worst). Team shares (target share, carry share, red zone share) use the team's totals from the same games.

What isn't available, because Sleeper doesn't carry the tracking data: routes run (so no yards per route run), evaded tackles, juke rate, EPA, and carries inside the 5. Sleeper also only records air yards on completed passes, so the usual aDOT, air yards share, WOPR, and RACR can't be computed honestly — the air-yard stats shown say exactly what they measure. Yards after contact and broken tackles run on a lower scale than PFF's charting, so compare players to each other here rather than to PFF numbers.

Trade Coach: negotiating

When you offer a trade on the Trade Calculator, the other manager's response is a prediction, worked out from their side of the deal — not an answer from the manager. Send the actual offer in Sleeper.

  • They accept based on how the deal looks from their side: trade value blended with what it does to their own starting lineup and timeline, using the same weights as the overall grade. How well a deal fits your plan doesn't change their answer. A contender giving up a top scorer for picks sees a worse deal than KTC value alone says. Their specific concerns count too: losing a position they're already thin at, giving up a top scorer while contending, a pick while rebuilding, taking on a position they're deep at or an older player in a rebuild, or having to drop someone. Each concern costs a few points of willingness; filling a need adds some. Managers who rarely trade want to come out ahead; active traders will take a little less. If the Trade Analysis reads the deal as bad for them, that counts against it too, though it isn't an automatic no. The market counts as well: when the deal is better or worse for them at market prices (what managers actually pay in completed trades) than at KTC, half that difference moves their willingness, up to 8 points either way, and the response says which player made the difference. Tested on about 136,000 completed Sleeper trades, each priced on its own day: it called more of them right than KTC alone.
  • Each response lists what they like and what gives them pause, then which side wins.
  • They answer in their own words, built from the same reasons: what bothers them most, then what they'd do instead.
  • Ask for something else keeps your side the same and looks through their roster for a different return: more value, a position, youth (24 or under), points now, picks (up to four), or players from one NFL team. It shows Fair deals they'd accept, best for you first. If none are Fair, it shows the one closest deal they'd take, labeled that way.
  • Their counter is the most realistic change they'd accept — you adding a piece they want, them keeping one of theirs, or them sending a lesser piece instead. Counters lean a few percent their way, like real ones do, but never past Fair for you.
  • If they'd only say yes because a deal now leans past Fair against you, the response says so.

After every response, a recommended next step lays out your options and highlights the one that leaves you best off on the overall grade:

  • Send it as is or accept their counter, when they'd say yes. These win near-ties, since a yes you already have beats one you're hoping for. Sending is never recommended when the deal is past Fair against you.
  • Counter back: one change that gets closer to even for you and that they'd still accept, like asking for one of their less important pieces, taking one of yours back out, or swapping in something cheaper they still want.
  • Shop it: the same piece offered to every other team in the league, checking every single player or pick and every pair they could send back. The best package a team would accept (and that's still Fair for them) is shown, and one click starts a new negotiation with that team.
  • Walk away: recommended when none of the above is Fair for you.

Their trading style comes from their completed trades on Trade Grades. The same offer always gets the same answer — there's no randomness.

Trade Coach: finding trades

Every Trade Coach option lists single trades to choose from, best for you first. Every one has to pass the same test as a Negotiate offer: the other manager would likely accept it, and it's Fair for you on the overall grade. If nobody would take a Fair offer (contenders rarely sell starters at even value), you'll see the cheapest deals they would take, flagged as Lopsided against you so the premium is visible. It never suggests an Unfair trade.

  • Best trades: the fairest trades between your team and every other team (one for one, two for one, either direction), kept to the ones that manager would likely accept, at most two per team.
  • Shop a player: each team's best offer for one of your players or picks, any return.
  • Get a player: offers of one, two, or three of your pieces the owner would take, one per lead piece so you get real alternatives.
  • Sell high and Buy low: your players and picks that sell for more than similar players in completed trades, each shopped for more than its KTC value; and players on other teams that sell for less, bought for less (see The market above).
  • Trade with a team: the best trades with one team you pick. Team Analyzer's "Find trades with them" opens it on that team.
  • Down-tier a player: pick one of your players and get back a lesser player plus extra pieces, so you gain total value.
  • Fix a position: players who out-score your weakest starter there by 2+ points a week, in trades where your best lineup gets better and the most valuable player in the deal comes to you. Your starters at other positions stay put; ones at this position can be part of the deal. Go all-in instead needs a gain of at least 3 points a week.
  • Rebuild: your players at or past the age their position usually starts losing value (RB 26, WR 28, TE 29, QB 30), each with the best return in picks and players 24 or under that any team would give.
  • Go all-in: starters paid for with picks, prospects, and bench players, ranked by how much they add to your best lineup. Your starters stay put.

Your timeline decides whether your side is graded as contending or rebuilding. Auto reads it from your roster. Pick Contending or Rebuilding to override it: at the top of Trade Coach for your team, or from the Timeline dropdown on either team in the Trade Calculator, so you can also tell it the other team has changed direction. Every grade on the page uses it, and the Trade Analysis marks it "set by you". Go all-in sets it to Contending, and Rebuild sets it to Rebuilding, so a rebuilding team going all-in isn't graded as spending picks its rebuild needed. It's saved in your browser.

Your side and Their side set rules every suggestion follows. On your side: pieces every trade must include (for Rebuild, the only players it shops, with offers from several teams), pieces you won't trade, and whole groups (a position, or picks) you won't trade. On their side: pieces a trade must bring back, which limits it to the teams that own them; pieces you don't want; and groups you won't take. Negotiate's "Ask for something else" skips anything you've said you don't want back.

Something here doesn't match what you're seeing? Changes to how grading works are always listed on Patch Notes.
