How We Grade

Every grade on The Vault can be traced back to numbers you can check yourself. This page explains where they come from and exactly how each verdict is decided. The figures shown are read live from the site's own settings, so they're always current.

Where the numbers come from

  • Player and pick values are KeepTradeCut's dynasty values, refreshed every night, in your league's format (superflex or 1QB, and your tight end premium).
  • Your league — rosters, records, schedule, scoring, and roster slots — comes straight from Sleeper.
  • Projected points are weekly projections scored with your league's actual scoring settings, not a generic PPR number.
  • Value history is a daily record of every value going back to 2020, which is what lets us price a past trade on the day it happened.

Fair, Lopsided, Unfair

A trade's gap is measured in % off: how far apart the two sides are, relative to the average side. Before comparing, both sides get KeepTradeCut's own consolidation adjustment — one great player is worth more than several decent ones that add up to the same raw value, because roster spots and starting slots are limited. That's the same math KTC's own trade calculator uses, so you can check any number here against it.

  • Fair: under off
  • Lopsided: to off
  • Unfair: or more off

Those cutoffs were calibrated against about 25,000 real dynasty trades from KeepTradeCut's trade database, so "Lopsided" means lopsided compared to what real leagues actually accept.

A Fair trade can be called out further: Great when it's also a strong fit for both teams, and Good when it's a strong fit for one. Fit can only add those labels to a trade that's already Fair.

The ± next to some grades is how much the less-established pieces (rookies, far-off picks) have swung in price over the last days — a reminder of how settled the number is.

Roster fit and timeline

The overall grade weighs three things. The three small bars under the main bar on the Trade Calculator and Trade Grades show each one.

  • KTC value () — the % off above.
  • Roster fit () — whether the trade fills a real positional need or opens one up, plus how much it moves each team's best possible starting lineup (projected points and VORP).
  • Timeline () — whether the ages and draft picks moving fit each team's plan (rebuilding teams should get younger and add picks; contenders can spend them), and whether it opens, widens, or closes a team's contention window.

The one-way rule: roster fit and timeline can make a trade's grade worse than KTC value alone, but never better. If KTC says a trade is lopsided, it reads lopsided here too — fit never talks a real value gap away. Each factor is also capped at so one extreme reading can't swamp the others. When fit does move the grade, both numbers are shown (for example "16% off · KTC 14%").

Grading past trades

Completed trades on Trade Grades are priced at each piece's value on the day the trade happened, not today's. A deal that was fair when it was made shouldn't read as unfair because a player broke out months later. Today's values are shown alongside ("3,180 → 2,678") so you can see how it's aged, and the Managers page shows each manager's net both ways: Net Then and Net Now.

Roster fit and timeline for past trades are judged against each team's current roster and standing.

VORP

Value Over Replacement Player is a player's projected points per game minus what you could get from the best player at the same position who wouldn't start for any team in your league. It makes positions comparable: a quarterback always scores more raw points, but VORP measures the edge over the alternative.

Replacement level is found by filling every team's starting lineup across your league — dedicated slots first, then each flex and superflex going to the best eligible player left — and taking the next player after the last starter at each position.

Playoff odds and contention windows

Projected record, playoff odds, and title odds come from simulating the rest of the season 2,000 times against your league's real remaining schedule, starting from everyone's actual record. Each team's weekly strength blends its best possible lineup's projected points with the points it has actually scored per game this season, both compared to the league average. Actual scoring counts more as the season goes on: weeks played ÷ (weeks played + 4), so a third after 2 weeks, half after 4, and two thirds after 8. Games vary about ±22% week to week. Median games count if your league plays them, and the week in progress is included until it's final.

A trade's effect this season (Trade Calculator and Trade Grades) runs the same simulation twice, 4,000 times each, on identical simulated games: once as things stand, and once with each team's weekly strength moved by what the trade does to its best lineup. Because the games are the same both times, the difference in playoff and title odds comes from the trade, not from luck in the simulation. On Trade Grades, "without" means the same team today with the trade undone; it's skipped when a player from the trade has since moved on.

Depth is what your lineup loses when a starter misses a game. For each starter, the site takes them out, refills your best lineup from the bench using your league's real slots (flex and superflex included, so a spare WR can cover an RB's bye if a flex can hold him), and measures the drop. Each starter is counted as out about 3.5 weeks a season, bye included. That's measured, not guessed: from 2018 to 2025, fantasy starters missed 2.4 games on average plus their bye, and it was nearly the same at every position. Because about 1 in 6 starters misses 6+ games, depth counts extra as insurance. A trade that raises that cost is marked down in roster fit, with a point of depth counting double a point of healthy-lineup gain, fully for contenders, 60% for flexible teams, and 30% for rebuilders. The This season odds use it at face value.

Will they take it? on the Trade Calculator reads the trade from the other manager's side: KTC value plus only their own lineup, needs, and timeline. Real managers don't need to come out ahead: across about 24,000 real dynasty trades in KTC's trade database, the typical one-for-one was about 13% off on KTC value. So a manager is expected to accept being up to about 12% behind, or whatever their own trades in your league show (a manager who never comes out behind stays strict; one who has taken lopsided deals is looser). Getting the best single player in a deal for more pieces counts in their favor, since real managers pay about 1.5× KTC value to consolidate. A trade that grades badly for them counts against it, and so does asking for a star straight up: in KTC's trade database only about 1 in 100 trades with an 8,000+ player was one-for-one (about 1 in 20 at 6,000 to 8,000). It's the same read the Offer button replies with. For a no, the suggested change is the smallest one they'd accept that still grades Fair for you; for a yes with room to spare, "You could ask for more" names the change that gets you the most while they'd still say yes and it stays Fair for them. The grade itself still uses KTC's own numbers.

Real trades under the grade shows what managers actually paid for the trade's main player, in your league's format (1QB or Superflex), from two sources that never overlap: KTC's trade database for its last few days (priced at today's values), and before that, about 90 days of real trades from Sleeper dynasty leagues that The Vault collects around the clock (each priced at KTC values from its own day, in its own league's format). Only trades where every piece has a price count, all with the same consolidation math as the grade. It uses trades from leagues as much like yours as it can: your exact settings (team count, Superflex or 1QB, PPR, TE premium) when there are at least 10 such trades, then any team count, then just your QB format and TE premium, then your QB format alone. The line says which it used. The Trade Database's market charts step down the same way (they need about 1,500 trades). It's always the loaded league's own settings, so it works the same for any league. It uses trades shaped like yours (him alone for two pieces, say) when there are at least 5; otherwise trades going the same direction (more pieces back, fewer, or the same number) where he was the main piece; otherwise any trade where he was the main piece. "Usually paid" is the middle half of those trades. It's there so you can check the grade against the market; it doesn't change the grade.

A team's contention window is how many of the next five years it projects as a real contender. This season counts when its playoff odds are 50% or better. Later years count when the roster's projected value (with players aging and picks arriving) stays near its peak and its lineup projects at or above the playoff line.

Manager styles

The trading styles on the Managers page come from simple rules you can check against a manager's own trades:

  • Consolidator — sends more pieces than they get back in 60%+ of deals. Depth builder — the reverse.
  • Youth & picks buyer — gets younger by half a year or more per deal on average, or nets picks in most deals. Win-now buyer — the reverse.
  • Value trader — no clear lean either way.
  • Active or Occasional is added when a manager trades 1.5× or ½× the league average.

Managers are ranked by Net Now: the value of all their trades at today's KTC prices. Net Then is the same trades at the prices on each trade's day, and the difference is how much their trades gained or lost afterward. A manager whose deals looked bad on the day but have aged well ranks higher, because buying before the market moves is the skill. Best and worst move are judged the same way.

Player stats on Compare Players

Weekly stats for every QB, RB, WR, and TE since 2018 come from Sleeper's box scores (charted by Sportradar). Every week is scored with your league's settings, so fantasy points, weekly finishes, "top-12 weeks," and "starter weeks" are all in your scoring — a starter week means finishing inside the number of players at that position your league actually starts.

The # next to a stat is its rank among every player at the same position over the exact games you picked. To be ranked, a player needs at least 40% as many of those games as the busiest player at the position, and rate stats (catch rate, yards per carry, …) also need at least a quarter of the top volume — so a two-catch game can't top the catch-rate list. The shading runs green (best) to red (worst). Team shares (target share, carry share, red zone share) use the team's totals from the same games.

What isn't available, because Sleeper doesn't carry the tracking data: routes run (so no yards per route run), evaded tackles, juke rate, EPA, and carries inside the 5. Sleeper also only records air yards on completed passes, so the usual aDOT, air yards share, WOPR, and RACR can't be computed honestly — the air-yard stats shown say exactly what they measure. Yards after contact and broken tackles run on a lower scale than PFF's charting, so compare players to each other here rather than to PFF numbers.

Trade Coach: negotiating

When you offer a trade on the Trade Calculator, the other manager's response is a prediction, worked out from their side of the deal — not the real manager. Send real offers in Sleeper.

  • They accept based on how the deal looks from their side: KTC value blended with what it does to their own starting lineup and timeline, using the same weights as the overall grade. How well a deal fits your plan doesn't change their answer. A contender giving up a top scorer for picks sees a worse deal than KTC value alone says. Their specific concerns count too: losing a position they're already thin at, giving up a top scorer while contending, a pick while rebuilding, taking on a position they're deep at or an older player in a rebuild, or having to drop someone. Each concern costs a few points of willingness; filling a need adds some. Managers who rarely trade want to come out ahead; active traders will take a little less. They never accept an offer the Trade Analysis would tell them to decline.
  • Each response lists what they like and what gives them pause, then which side wins.
  • They answer in their own words, built from the same reasons: what bothers them most, then what they'd do instead.
  • Ask for something else keeps your side the same and looks through their roster for a different return: more value, a position, youth (24 or under), points now, picks (up to four), or players from one NFL team. It shows Fair deals they'd accept, best for you first. If none are Fair, it shows the one closest deal they'd take, labeled that way.
  • Their counter is the most realistic change they'd accept — you adding a piece they want, them keeping one of theirs, or them sending a lesser piece instead. Counters lean a few percent their way, like real ones do, but never past Fair for you.
  • If they'd only say yes because a deal now leans past Fair against you, the response says so.

After every response, a recommended next step lays out your options and highlights the one that leaves you best off on the overall grade:

  • Send it as is or accept their counter, when they'd say yes. These win near-ties, since a yes you already have beats one you're hoping for. Sending is never recommended when the deal is past Fair against you.
  • Counter back: one change that gets closer to even for you and that they'd still accept, like asking for one of their less important pieces, taking one of yours back out, or swapping in something cheaper they still want.
  • Shop it: the same piece offered to every other team in the league, checking every single player or pick and every pair they could send back. The best package a team would accept (and that's still Fair for them) is shown, and one click starts a new negotiation with that team.
  • Walk away: recommended when none of the above is Fair for you.

Their trading style comes from their real trade history on Trade Grades. The same offer always gets the same answer — there's no randomness.

Trade Coach: finding trades

Best trades, Shop a player, Get a player, Fix a position, Rebuild, and Go all-in each list single trades to choose from, best for you first. Every one has to pass the same test as a Negotiate offer: the other manager would likely accept it, and it's Fair for you on the overall grade. If nobody would take a Fair offer (contenders rarely sell starters at even value), you'll see the cheapest deals they would take, flagged as Lopsided against you so the premium is visible. It never suggests an Unfair trade.

  • Best trades: the fairest trades between your team and every other team (one for one, two for one, either direction), kept to the ones that manager would likely accept, at most two per team.
  • Shop a player: each team's best offer for one of your players or picks, any return.
  • Get a player: offers of one, two, or three of your pieces the owner would take, one per lead piece so you get real alternatives.
  • Fix a position: players who out-score your weakest starter there by 2+ points a week, in trades where your best lineup gets better and the most valuable player in the deal comes to you. Your starters at other positions stay put; ones at this position can be part of the deal. Go all-in instead needs a gain of at least 3 points a week.
  • Rebuild: your players at or past the age their position usually starts losing value (RB 26, WR 28, TE 29, QB 30), each with the best return in picks and players 24 or under that any team would give.
  • Go all-in: starters paid for with picks, prospects, and bench players, ranked by how much they add to your best lineup. Your starters stay put.

Your timeline decides whether your side is graded as contending or rebuilding. Auto reads it from your roster. Pick Contending or Rebuilding to override it: at the top of Trade Coach for your team, or from the Timeline dropdown on either team in the Trade Calculator, so you can also tell it the other team has changed direction. Every grade on the page uses it, and the Trade Analysis marks it "set by you". Go all-in sets it to Contending, and Rebuild sets it to Rebuilding, so a rebuilding team going all-in isn't graded as spending picks its rebuild needed. It's saved in your browser.

Your side and Their side set rules every suggestion follows. On your side: pieces every trade must include (for Rebuild, the only players it shops, with offers from several teams), pieces you won't trade, and whole groups (a position, or picks) you won't trade. On their side: pieces a trade must bring back, which limits it to the teams that own them; pieces you don't want; and groups you won't take. Negotiate's "Ask for something else" skips anything you've said you don't want back.
