How We Grade

Every grade on The Vault can be traced back to numbers you can check yourself. This page explains where they come from and exactly how each verdict is decided. The figures shown are read live from the site's own settings, so they're always current.

Where the numbers come from

Values come from KeepTradeCut, leagues from Sleeper and injury details from ESPN, all scored to your league.

How it works
  • Player and pick values are KeepTradeCut's dynasty values, refreshed every night, in your league's format (superflex or 1QB, and your tight end premium). A pick is priced at the slot it should land in (early, mid or late): next year's draft by each team's projected final record (its real record so far plus the rest of the season, simulated), later drafts by lineup strength. Early and late 1sts and 2nds are KTC's mid price times the spread managers really pay for them. Every page, chart and past grade prices picks this way.
  • Injury details (the specific injury, return date and last update) come from ESPN once a day; injury status comes from Sleeper.
  • Your league — rosters, records, schedule, scoring, and roster slots — comes straight from Sleeper.
  • Projected points are weekly projections scored with your league's actual scoring settings, not a generic PPR number.
  • Value history is a daily record of every value going back to 2020, which is what lets us price a past trade on the day it happened.

Good trade or bad trade?

One answer for your team: is this trade good for you, judged on value, fit, your timeline and, if you're contending, this season's points.

Model update (November 22, 2026): Additive market value now drives trade prices and the verdict's piece-value comparison, while measured roster impacts and timeline tilts remain separate. Older percentages in the historical discussion below describe the prior version and have not yet been revalidated for this new grading formula.

How it works

The Trade Calculator leads with one answer for your team, on a ladder: Great trade, Good trade, Slight edge, Toss-up, Leans bad, Bad trade or Terrible trade, with one sentence saying why and one line with that tier's real track record for your timeline. Right under it is the other team's verdict on the same ladder, because a trade that's great for you isn't one they'll take if it reads as bad for them: in 1.3 million accepted trades, when one side's verdict was Good or better, the other side's was a Toss-up or better only about a third of the time. When it's good for you but Leans bad or worse for them, the headline says One-sided instead, with both verdicts underneath; when it's good for you and at least a Toss-up for them, it's marked Works for both. It weighs what you'd gain or give up in value, how the trade fits your roster and your timeline, and, if you're playing to win now, what it does to your lineup this season. Contending, this season counts heavily (2 points of score for every point a week your lineup gains or loses, plus half the value-and-fit read); rebuilding, it's value, how the trade fits your roster, and your timeline: getting younger and adding picks counts (at an even price, rebuilding teams that got much younger were still ahead on value later 62% of the time, against 43% for those that got older); a flexible timeline sits in between. Your timeline can be switched right next to the verdict, because the same trade can be good for a rebuilder and bad for a contender.

Example: Ja'Marr Chase and Joe Burrow for Patrick Mahomes, George Pickens and three 1sts. You'd get about 10% more value, but as the league favorite it costs you 3.9 points a week and about 5 points of title odds: on the losing side contending, on the winning side rebuilding. The price grade below (Fair, Lopsided, Unfair) still says whether the price is fair for both teams, but it's supporting detail now. Trade Coach reads every trade with the same score, at prices managers actually pay, and lists trades that are good for you first, then Toss-ups, then the rest.

Where the tiers come from. Real trades in completed 2025 and 2024 Sleeper dynasty leagues (1,342,426 trades from 64,898 leagues, every format), each judged as its league stood that day with that week's projections, then checked against what happened. A score just past even turned out to be a coin flip, so the tiers sit where real outcomes actually separate: +25 and up is a Great trade (contenders did 7.2 points better against their playoff odds than the average contender who traded; 87% of rebuilding teams were still ahead on value a year or more later), +15 a Good trade, +6 a Slight edge (70% of rebuilding teams still ahead later), −6 to +6 a Toss-up (49%: a true coin flip), then Leans bad, Bad trade (below −15) and Terrible trade (below −25; contenders did 16 points worse, and 13% of rebuilding teams were still ahead). Each tier came out in the same order in 2024 and in 2025, and in Superflex and 1QB. The line under the verdict quotes your tier's record for your timeline. Contending: how much better or worse teams did against their playoff odds the day of the trade, since a contender's verdict is mostly this season's points, compared with the average contender who traded (teams that trade at all beat their odds by about 2 points, so the odds alone would flatter every tier). Rebuilding or flexible: the share still ahead on value a year or more later, with a draft pick that has since been used counted as the rookie it became (the average player taken in that round of real rookie drafts). The lineup math's predicted points showed up at 91% with real weekly projections. Two things were tested and left out because they didn't help: knocking contenders for buying older players (contenders who did that came out ahead more often, since sellers price vets to move) and for a Superflex team selling its 3rd QB (the lineup and depth math already catch it).

Out of the playoff race. During the season, a team with under 5% playoff odds gets no credit or blame for this season's points in its verdict, whatever its timeline. In 2024 and 2025 trades, for teams that far out, the points part of the score said almost nothing about how the trade worked out for playoffs or titles, and it made the score worse at telling who'd come out ahead on value. Those teams sold 5+ points a week in 24% of their trades, playoff-bound contenders in 2%. Trade Grades applies the same rule to this season's completed trades, with each team's odds as of the week it traded: the real standings up to then, and the rest of the season played out from there.

How the verdict counts value. In the Trade Calculator and Trade Coach, the verdict reads each player and pick at its price, all KTC, with two adjustments. Bigger pieces count for more and smaller ones for less: price × (price ÷ 5,000)0.25, so a 9,000 player counts as about 10,400 and a 2,000 one as about 1,590. And by position and age: QBs +6%, RBs +2%, TEs −7%, picks −5%, about +0.7% for each year past 25 (up to 33), +1.6% more for an RB, WR or TE 27 or older, and −2% for a QB 32 or older (the age-27 cliff below still applies to the trade's best piece). Tested on 3.2 million team-sides of 2025 and 2026 trades against the verdict it replaced, which blended in values learned from trades: in 2025 it was better at picking the teams that beat their playoff odds (rank correlation 5.2 vs 4.3) and gained points a week (8.6 vs 7.4), and in 2026 at value since the trade (63.8 vs 59.7; rebuilding teams 70.4 vs 62.2), while value later in 2025 slipped a little (39.0 vs 40.6). The price you see (Fair, Lopsided, Unfair) stays on KTC and what players trade for, so you can still check it on KTC, and completed trades on Trade Grades stay graded on KTC only.

What the price misses. Checked on 2.7 million team-sides from 2024 and 2025 trades, comparing each side's value at the trade with its value later. A value edge holds: a side that won by 10-20% was still up a median 10.5% later, with no drift back toward even. But at the same KTC price, picks gained on players: the side that got only picks came out ahead 59% of the time a year later and 77% two years on, probably because KTC's scale is relative and each new rookie class pushes existing players down. And turning several pieces into one cheap player lost: consolidating into a best piece worth under 3,000 came out ahead only 39% of the time (for better players, 2-for-1s came out even). Age 27 is a cliff for RBs, WRs and TEs: at the same price, a side whose best piece was that age came out behind (RBs 10% two years on and 5% one year on, WRs 8% / 5%, TEs 7% / 8%). And in straight swaps, the side getting the WR came out about 8% behind in both seasons. So the verdict counts picks about 5% above their KTC value, a cheap consolidation up to 10% below (fading with the player's value: about the full 10% under 2,500, half at 4,000, under 2% past 6,000, so two players a few points apart never land on opposite sides of a cutoff; at a Fair price, upgrades into a best piece under 4,000 came out ahead later only 28% of the time), a 27+ RB, WR or TE 10% below for rebuilding and flexible teams and 5% below for contenders, and a WR 3% below: the sizes that best predicted value later in each season on its own, set under what was measured where it could be a market cycle (WRs). Flexible teams get all of these at half strength, the cut below included: on 313,576 flexible team-sides from 2025 trades, at full strength their verdict tracked value later best (rank correlation 49) but barely tracked playoffs against their odds (3, against 11 without any) or weekly points (8, against 18); at half it's 44, 7 and 13, in between like the timeline itself. Contenders lost almost nothing on playoffs with them (10.8 to 10.1), so they keep theirs.For rebuilding and flexible teams, getting fewer pieces than you send counts 6% below the price at any value (and getting more, 6% above): Slight edge or better trades that consolidated ended 10%+ behind later 25% of the time, against 6% for the side that spread out. Contenders don't get this cut: consolidating into a better starter helps this season. Contenders keep the smaller cut because a stronger one made their verdict track playoffs less well: veterans do help this season. Followed four years, 2021-2023 player trades bear it out: RBs and WRs 27-28 lost 15-17% (TEs that age held their value). None of these was already in timeline fit. Young TEs gained too, but only in one of the two seasons, so they're left out. The price (Fair, Lopsided, Unfair) stays on KTC, so you can still check it there; the calculator says under the verdict when one of these moved it. Also tested and left out: counting predicted points less early in the season (only 61% of the predicted gain showed up in weeks 1-4, but counting it less made the verdict worse at predicting playoffs).

Tested on real trades (the first test, of the earlier three answers: Good trade at +3, Bad trade at −3). Every two-team trade in 2,924 completed 2025 Sleeper dynasty leagues (27,327 trades), each judged as its league stood that day and checked against what happened next. In-season, the side the verdict called a Good trade scored 5.7 more points a week than its partner afterward, and the Bad trade side 6.3 fewer; the lineup math's predicted change showed up at 79%. Contenders with a Good trade made the playoffs 86% of the time against 82% expected, and with a Bad trade 66% against 68%. Sides called Good were still ahead on value a year later 69% of the time. What it doesn't do is predict acceptance: managers accept trades it calls Bad for them all the time (only 18% of real trades left both teams not worse), so whether a trade is likely to happen comes from the market's price ranges instead.

Trade price: Fair, Lopsided, or Unfair

Vault's primary price now counts each player and pick at its full individual market value. A package-consolidation curve cannot quietly erase a second player.

How the new price works

1. Start with individual market prices. We use each asset's KTC value in your league's quarterback and tight-end format, adjusted by observed player/pick trade premiums in recent completed deals when enough data exists. Every asset is then counted fully. Without sufficient market observations, it stays at its KTC value. A 2,000-value player added to a trade increases the price of that side by 2,000, even when the two lead players are nearly equal in value.

2. Separate price from team utility. The trade's price measures what the assets are worth on a shared market scale. Independently, each team's recommendation considers the actual before/after best lineup, depth, positional needs, age, contention window and picks. Consolidation can help a contender improve its starting lineup or hurt a rebuilder who sells depth, but those are roster/timeline effects, not an invented dollar bonus on the opposing player's price. A manager's willingness to accept is a separate unconfirmed simulation, not a probability.

3. The reason we changed. On the November 22 historical benchmark, 2024 player-only one-for-two trades agreed with the greater additive market value a year later 82.8% of the time using additive prices, versus 62.1% under Vault's old full consolidation curve. In 2025, the figures were 86.4% versus 63.5%. For the 1,245 player-only trades with lead pieces within 5% of each other, the additive model matched the future market-value direction 92.2% of the time versus 83.2% for the full curve. These compare future additive KTC price direction in completed trades, not acceptance, actual lineup impact, or future championships. The sample can overlap past curve training, so it is not an independent proof of a universally fair trading rule.

Concrete example. In Superflex with TE premium, Denzel Boston plus Corey Kiner for George Kittle is approximately 35% apart on additive market prices, while the legacy curve called the same package only 1.4% apart. It effectively attributed only about 37 of Kiner's ~1,863 adjusted market points to that particular package. Under the redesigned calculator, the full market-price gap determines the price grade. The old curve remains in the price comparison for transparency but does not decide the verdict, balancing suggestions, Trade Coach price checks, or new Trade Grades.

How the labels work. Price Fair currently means less than % apart, Lopsided means % to %, and Unfair means % or more. These cutoffs are provisional. The numbers were originally chosen using a different package-price distribution; we've retained them temporarily for continuity, not claimed a fresh calibration. A Fair label is not a guarantee a manager will accept the deal, and a Lopsided deal is not necessarily a bad roster move for the team paying more.

What happens next. We will reassess both the label cutoffs and the manager model against independent, time-separated outcomes. Historical verdict-tier success percentages based on the former value formula are withheld from the revised calculator until revalidated.

Roster fit and timeline

Fit can make a grade worse, never better, so a bad price always reads as a bad price.

How it works

The overall grade weighs three things. The three small bars under the Trade Calculator's main bar show each one. Historical Trade Grades use price only because exact rosters and projections from each trade date are not reliably available.

  • Trade value () — the % off above.
  • Roster fit () — whether the trade fills a real positional need or opens one up, plus how much it moves each team's best possible starting lineup (projected points and VORP).
  • Timeline () — whether the ages and draft picks moving fit each team's plan (rebuilding teams should get younger and add picks; contenders can spend them), and whether it opens, widens, or closes a team's contention window.

The one-way rule: roster fit and timeline can make a trade's grade worse than trade value alone, but never better. If trade value says a trade is lopsided, it reads lopsided here too — fit never talks a real value gap away. Each factor is also capped at so one extreme reading can't swamp the others. When fit does move the grade, both numbers are shown (for example "16% off · KTC 14%").

Grading past trades

Past trades are judged at each piece's price the day they happened, so a later breakout doesn't rewrite the grade.

How it works

Completed trades on Trade Grades are measured from each asset's price at the date of the trade when price history exists. When one asset lacks that history, the entire deal is clearly marked as using today's prices instead. Trade Grades uses KTC values for these completed deals; it does not apply the proposed-trade market premium to the past. Today's prices appear next to the historical prices so managers can see how the assets aged.

Archived roster evidence (first phase). For trades after each season's latest completed draft, Vault now replays recorded completed player moves backward from that season's archived rosters. If those moves reconcile, the Trade Grades details show provisional pre-trade player counts for both teams. If transactions are missing, a draft lies between the trade and the available roster, or the replay cannot reconcile a move, it reports the gap rather than claiming a past roster. These counts do not affect grading.

Historical lineup, roster-fit, and timeline effects are not graded. We cannot reliably reconstruct each manager's exact roster, plan, projections, and standings at the moment of every archived transaction. Using today's roster would produce a false historical verdict. The live Trade Calculator still evaluates both teams' fit and playoff changes before a proposed trade.

VORP

Points per game above the best player who wouldn't start in your league, so positions compare fairly.

How it works

Value Over Replacement Player is a player's projected points per game minus what you could get from the best player at the same position who wouldn't start for any team in your league. It makes positions comparable: a quarterback always scores more raw points, but VORP measures the edge over the alternative.

Replacement level is found by filling every team's starting lineup across your league — dedicated slots first, then each flex and superflex going to the best eligible player left — and taking the next player after the last starter at each position.

Playoff odds and contention windows

Your remaining schedule played out 4,000 times, with injuries and byes counted and real scores counting more as the season goes.

How it works

What if he's out (Team Analyzer) runs the same simulation on the same random games, with that starter gone and your best lineup rebuilt each week, so the difference is the player alone. Schedule left uses each opponent's projected lineup for that week.

Projected record, playoff odds, and title odds come from simulating the rest of the season 4,000 times against your league's real remaining schedule, starting from everyone's actual record. Each week is played at that week's strength: the best lineup from who Sleeper projects to play that week, at that week's matchups, blended with the points the team has actually scored per game this season, both compared to the league average. The playoff bracket uses the playoff weeks' lineups. Actual scoring counts more as the season goes on: weeks played ÷ (weeks played + 4), so a third after 2 weeks, half after 4, and two thirds after 8. Games vary about ±22% week to week. Median games count if your league plays them, and the week in progress starts from the points already scored: once its first game kicks off, starters whose NFL team has played keep their real points, and only the starters still to play are simulated. The odds are then corrected by how often teams given them really got there. Checked on 949,362 team-sides from 2024 and 2025 dynasty trades, each with the odds given that day: teams given 30% to make the playoffs made it about 30% of the time (an average miss of under 1 point; about 1 point for title odds).

A proposed trade's effect this season (Trade Calculator) runs the same simulation twice, 4,000 times each, on identical simulated games: once as things stand, and once with each team's strength moved, week by week, by what the trade does to that week's best lineup. Because the games are the same both times, the difference in playoff and title odds comes from the trade, not from luck in the simulation. On Trade Grades, "without" means the same team today with the trade undone; it's skipped when a player from the trade has since moved on.

Injuries and byes. A player's projected points per game is what he scores in the games he plays, from Sleeper's projections for the rest of the season, so it follows his current role rather than what was projected for weeks already played. Projections refresh about every hour. During the season, this season's lineup is built week by week for every week left, playoffs included, from the players Sleeper projects to play that week, each adjusted for that week's matchup (his projection that week against his average, kept within 0.6 to 1.5 times), then averaged. Byes count for real, and so do known injuries: a quarterback out for the season after knee surgery adds nothing to whoever trades for him, and a receiver back in week 8 counts from week 8. When ESPN lists a return date, that week is used instead of Sleeper's, and a player on IR can't count before his four-game minimum, with a bye not counting as a game. The Trade Calculator tags these players ("Q", "Out, back week 8 (hamstring)", "IR (min wk 13, exp wk 13)"), hovering a tag or tapping it on a phone shows the details, and it shows how many weeks each new starter would start. A player on IR with no return date whom Sleeper doesn't project for any week counts as out for the weeks left. Dynasty value is unchanged: KTC prices the injury in.

Depth is what your lineup loses when a starter misses a game. For each starter, the site takes them out, refills your best lineup from the bench using your league's real slots (flex and superflex included, so a spare WR can cover an RB's bye if a flex can hold him), and measures the drop. Each starter is counted as out about 3.5 weeks a season, bye included. That's measured, not guessed: from 2018 to 2025, fantasy starters missed 2.4 games on average plus their bye, and it was nearly the same at every position. During the season the bye and known injuries are already in the week-by-week lineup, so depth covers the injuries nobody knows about yet: 2.5 games per starter. Because about 1 in 6 starters misses 6+ games, depth counts extra as insurance. A trade that raises that cost is marked down in roster fit, with a point of depth counting double a point of healthy-lineup gain, fully for contenders, 60% for flexible teams, and 30% for rebuilders. The This season odds use it at face value.

Their best player. Managers rarely trade their cornerstones. In 34,421 real 2024 and 2025 trade sides, with each roster as it stood that day, the best player a team sent was its most valuable in 8% of its trades (contending teams 5%, rebuilding 10%, flexible 12%) and one of its top 3 in 23% (contending 16%). When one did move, the team got no premium for it, so what a reluctant owner would take can't be read from trades that happened. Trade Coach tags a team's best player, and a contending team's top 3, as rarely traded rather than hiding them, and the Trade Calculator says when a trade includes one.

Offer on the Trade Calculator reads the trade from the other manager's side: trade value plus only their own lineup, needs, and timeline. Managers don't need to come out ahead, and how far behind they'll go depends on the shape of the deal. Across 1.3 million accepted trades in 2024 and 2025, the side that came out behind typically went 13-21% behind in a one-for-one (further when picks are in it: pick prices are a matter of opinion), but only 5-9% when pieces are added on either side. So a manager is expected to accept being as far behind as the typical manager in their spot (the shape of the deal from their side, whether picks are in it, and how valuable the best piece is), never more than 20%, or whatever their own trades in your league show (a manager who never comes out behind stays strict; one who has taken lopsided deals is looser). Trade Coach uses the same line: it doesn't suggest a trade that puts the other team further behind than managers in their spot usually accept. Getting the best single player in a deal for more pieces counts a little in their favor: on trade value, which already prices consolidating, teams that did so accepted being about 1% behind. A trade that grades badly for them counts against it, and so does asking for a star straight up: in KTC's trade database only about 1 in 100 trades with an 8,000+ player was one-for-one (about 1 in 20 at 6,000 to 8,000). For a no, their counter is the smallest change they'd accept that still grades Fair for you. Trade Coach uses the same read to pick trades they'd likely take. None of it changes the grade.

Contend or rebuild (Team Analyzer) lays out four paths for a team. Push now's example trade buys the player who adds the most to its lineup from a team out of the race, offering picks and bench players under 27 until the two sides are about even on trade value, and it opens in the Trade Calculator so you can check its grade. Players it can't reach that way aren't listed.

A team's contention window is how many of the next five years it projects as a real contender. This season counts when its playoff odds are 50% or better. Later years count when the roster's projected value (with players aging and picks arriving) stays near its peak and its lineup projects at or above the playoff line. A pick that has arrived plays like the rookies taken at its slot really did: across 61,730 rookie drafts of the 2023-2025 classes, the player taken at a mid 1st averaged 10.9 PPR points a game as a rookie and 13.0 the next year, a late 1st 9.9, a mid 2nd 6.5 (scaled to each league's scoring). It used to count a pick's value at the league's going rate per point, which made a late 1st a 13.6-point starter in its first year.

The market

What managers really pay in completed trades, next to KTC value, as one price on every page.

How it works

The market is what managers actually pay in completed dynasty trades: where a player trades above or below his KTC value. Since 9 October 2026 the Trade Calculator's grade uses it, each piece at what it actually trades for (see Fair, Lopsided, Unfair); completed trades on Trade Grades and Managers are won and lost on KTC values alone; the values shown on the page stay KTC's.

The market under the grade shows what managers actually paid for the trade's main player, in your league's format (1QB or Superflex), from two sources that never overlap: KTC's trade database for its last few days (priced at today's values), and before that, completed trades from Sleeper dynasty leagues that The Vault collects around the clock, up to 30,000 spread evenly over the rest of the last 45 days (each priced at KTC values from its own day, in its own league's format). Only trades where every piece has a price count, all with the same consolidation math as the grade. It uses trades from leagues as much like yours as it can: your exact settings (team count, Superflex or 1QB, PPR, TE premium) when there are at least 10 such trades, then any team count, then just your QB format and TE premium, then your QB format alone. The line says which it used. Trade Search's market charts step down the same way (they need about 1,500 trades). It's always the loaded league's own settings, so it works the same for any league.

Market value (every page: player cards, Player Rankings, Compare, the Player Market page, the Trade Calculator and the grade, one number) is what managers actually pay for a player: in every completed trade he headlined in your league's format over the last 45 days (from KTC's trade database and the Sleeper dynasty trades The Vault collects), what the team getting him sent compared with what his side was worth, both on trade value (so the normal premium for getting the better single piece is already counted, and a star isn't read as overpriced just for being one piece), as a median, applied to his KTC value today. Every trade shape counts: across all of them each KTC value level comes out about even with KTC, so the median isn't tilted toward stars or cheap players. (Like-for-like trades alone read every star low, since a star's partner can only be worth less, and consolidation trades alone read cheap players high. Getting a star for more pieces costs only about 6% beyond KTC's own consolidation bonus.) Trades from when his KTC value was more than 1.5 times higher or lower than today's are left out: a premium measured on a different price says little about this one (a backup who suddenly started went from a KTC value of 17 to 827, and his old "9,806% over" would have priced him at 9,157). It needs at least 5 such trades. The usual range shown beside it, and the trades listed under his chart, come from leagues as much like yours as there are enough of. Charts draw it over time as the price the grade used on each date. Market highs & lows on the Player Market page compare each player with others at the same position and KTC value instead, so a whole position trading under KTC (tight ends do, against KTC's TE-premium prices) doesn't fill the list.

Over or under similar players. A bare "12% over" or "8% under" anywhere on the site is against his KTC value. Against players like him it always says so, in its label or its section's heading: "12% over similar players" means he goes for 12% more than players at his position and KTC value (within about 15%) go for. That matters because KTC squeezes its low end: under about 2,000 nearly every player goes for more than KTC (the median is about 20% over, since the cheapest piece you can send back is usually worth more), so "over KTC" said little about the player himself. Measured against players like him, cheap players split over and under like everyone else. The price itself is still what he actually goes for, and the grade uses it. Your market, Sell high and Buy low rank this way. Movers (Player Market) shows Market highs & lows and Rising & cooling: how far each player's market price (against KTC) moved since the prices of about a month ago, the same prices past trades are graded with, ranked by how big that move is for players at his value, since cheap players' prices swing more. It starts at a KTC value of 1,000 and leaves out players whose KTC value moved more than 1.5 times, or whose price was more than double or under half of KTC's, where KTC was catching up to a sudden role change.

Market prices in the grade are rebuilt every day for each league format (Superflex or 1QB, and TE premium) from the same trades as the market value above. A piece whose KTC value has risen 20% or more since those trades is left at KTC, because managers paid about 6% under the old read on 18,405 such trades. Past trades on Trade Grades use the market prices of their own time: a snapshot on the 1st and 16th of every month since October 2023, each from the 45 days of completed trades before it, so a trade is never graded with what happened later. Tested week by week on 275,273 trades from June to October 2026 with the prices rebuilt each week: 77.2% graded Fair against 75.0% on KTC's values (1-for-1 trades, 57% against 54%), and using all of the market price did better than any part of it.

The trades behind it (under a player's chart on Player Market) lists the completed trades his market value comes from, newest first, so you can check the number yourself. The most extreme tenth at each end (lopsided or partly recorded trades) is faded, since a median barely moves for them.

Your market (top of Player Market) shows which of your players and picks go for more or less than similar players, and which moved most since the prices of about a month ago. Sell high and Buy low in Trade Coach act on it: sell what goes for more than players like it, for more than its KTC value, and buy what goes for less, for less.

Who pays up (Managers) shows what each manager in your league has paid over or under market, by the position of the best piece they got. Sell high shows the managers who pay up for a position first.

How it does on real trades: see Does It Work?, including where KTC's calculator does better.

Rookie drafts (Trade Lab) shows where each rookie actually went in Sleeper dynasty rookie drafts in your QB format, and what each pick slot bought: KTC's value for the pick on draft day next to the draft-day KTC value of the players taken there.

How often he changes hands (player card, Player Market lookup) counts the completed trades a player was part of in the last 30 days, next to the 10 players at his position closest to his KTC value. It's how often, not how easily: stars change hands less than the players just below them because their owners rarely sell, not because nobody's buying. Picks are left out, since they're tracked by year and round with every tier together.

Does the market predict KTC? We checked: across about 35,000 weekly reads from 2022 to 2026, players managers were paying well above KTC for didn't go on to gain more KTC value over the next 30 or 60 days than anyone else (about half rose more than the market either way, in every band). So "the market" here is what you can get in a trade now, not a forecast of where KTC is headed.

Manager styles

Styles come from simple rules on each manager's own trades, and managers are ranked by value at the time of each trade.

How it works

The trading styles on the Managers page come from simple rules you can check against a manager's own trades:

  • Consolidator — sends more pieces than they get back in 60%+ of deals. Depth builder — the reverse.
  • Youth & picks buyer — gets younger by half a year or more per deal on average, or nets picks in most deals. Win-now buyer — the reverse.
  • Value trader — no clear lean either way.
  • Active or Occasional is added when a manager trades 1.5× or ½× the league average.

Managers are ranked by Net Then: the value of all their trades at the KTC prices on each trade's day. Net Now is the same trades at today's prices, and the difference is how much their trades gained or lost afterward. We used to rank by Net Now, on the idea that buying before the market moves is the skill. The data says otherwise. Across 2.7 million trade-sides, a manager's edge on price in some trades predicted their edge in their other trades (correlation 0.14) and the next season (0.11; the top fifth one season averaged 1.3% ahead the next, the bottom fifth 1.4% behind). How their trades moved afterward didn't carry over at all (0.01). Getting good prices is a skill; the rest is mostly luck.

What 2.7 million trades say

There is no deadline premium, trading helps teams that need it, and picks gain on players over time.

How it works

From every two-team trade in 64,898 Sleeper dynasty leagues in 2024 and 2025, each judged as its league stood that day, then followed to the end of the season and beyond.

  • There's no deadline premium. What contenders paid stayed within 1% of an even price in every month and every week of the season, deadline weeks included. Prices don't get better or worse with the calendar.
  • Trading helps teams that need it. Among teams with a losing record the season before, those that made no trades won the title 3% of the time and made the playoffs 34%; those with 15 or more trades, 8% and 45%. Teams that never traded did worst at every level. (Part of this is that active managers are engaged managers.)
  • Rebuilds take more than a year, but picking a lane beats the middle. Teams that rebuilt in 2024 made the 2025 playoffs 40% of the time and won 5% of titles; contenders, 69% and 14%; teams in between, 35% and 4%.
  • Picks gain on players over time. Bought at an even price, picks came out ahead 59% of the time a year later and 77% two years on, whatever the month they were bought.

Player stats on Compare Players

Weekly stats since 2018 in your scoring, each ranked against players at the same position.

How it works

Weekly stats for every QB, RB, WR, and TE since 2018 come from Sleeper's box scores (charted by Sportradar). Every week is scored with your league's settings, so fantasy points, weekly finishes, "top-12 weeks," and "starter weeks" are all in your scoring — a starter week means finishing inside the number of players at that position your league actually starts.

The # next to a stat is its rank among every player at the same position over the exact games you picked. To be ranked, a player needs at least 40% as many of those games as the busiest player at the position, and rate stats (catch rate, yards per carry, …) also need at least a quarter of the top volume — so a two-catch game can't top the catch-rate list. The shading runs green (best) to red (worst). Team shares (target share, carry share, red zone share) use the team's totals from the same games.

What isn't available, because Sleeper doesn't carry the tracking data: routes run (so no yards per route run), evaded tackles, juke rate, EPA, and carries inside the 5. Sleeper also only records air yards on completed passes, so the usual aDOT, air yards share, WOPR, and RACR can't be computed honestly — the air-yard stats shown say exactly what they measure. Yards after contact and broken tackles run on a lower scale than PFF's charting, so compare players to each other here rather than to PFF numbers.

Trade Coach: negotiating

A prediction of how the other manager would answer, built from their side of the deal. It's not their real answer.

How it works

Four separate questions, not one score. The calculator now distinguishes current market fairness (additive prices and the full package adjustment), each team's roster-specific benefit, and the other manager's simulated willingness. A trade can be a good price and a poor fit, or appear acceptable to the model while still being bad for the recipient. Those are different judgments, and Vault points out when they disagree.

How much history supports the response? Coach reports whether the manager has no usable, very small, limited or meaningful league-trade history. These are sample-size descriptions, not a calibrated confidence interval or an acceptance probability. Completed trades reveal what managers accepted, not all the offers they rejected; we cannot honestly estimate the chance that a specific future offer gets accepted from those records alone.

When you offer a trade on the Trade Calculator, the other manager's response is a prediction, worked out from their side of the deal — not an answer from the manager. Send the actual offer in Sleeper.

  • They accept based on how the deal looks from their side: trade value blended with what it does to their own starting lineup and timeline, using the same weights as the overall grade. How well a deal fits your plan doesn't change their answer. A contender giving up a top scorer for picks sees a worse deal than KTC value alone says. Their specific concerns count too: losing a position they're already thin at, giving up a top scorer while contending, a pick while rebuilding, taking on a position they're deep at or an older player in a rebuild, or having to drop someone. Each concern costs a few points of willingness; filling a need adds some. Managers who rarely trade want to come out ahead; active traders will take a little less. If the Trade Analysis reads the deal as bad for them, that counts against it too, though it isn't an automatic no. The market counts as well: when the deal is better or worse for them at market prices (what managers actually pay in completed trades) than at KTC, half that difference moves their willingness, up to 8 points either way, and the response says which player made the difference. Tested on about 136,000 completed Sleeper trades, each priced on its own day: it called more of them right than KTC alone.
  • Each response lists what they like and what gives them pause, then which side wins.
  • They answer in their own words, built from the same reasons: what bothers them most, then what they'd do instead.
  • Ask for something else keeps your side the same and looks through their roster for a different return: more value, a position, youth (24 or under), points now, picks (up to four), or players from one NFL team. It shows Fair deals they'd accept, best for you first. If none are Fair, it shows the one closest deal they'd take, labeled that way.
  • Their counter is the most realistic change they'd accept — you adding a piece they want, them keeping one of theirs, or them sending a lesser piece instead. Counters lean a few percent their way, like real ones do, but never past Fair for you.
  • If they'd only say yes because a deal now leans past Fair against you, the response says so.

After every response, a recommended next step lays out your options and highlights the one that leaves you best off on the overall grade:

  • Send this offer as is or accept their counter, when they'd say yes. These win near-ties, since a yes you already have beats one you're hoping for. Sending is never recommended when the deal is past Fair against you.
  • Counter back: one change that gets closer to even for you and that they'd still accept, like asking for one of their less important pieces, taking one of yours back out, or swapping in something cheaper they still want.
  • Shop it: the same piece offered to every other team in the league, checking every single player or pick and every pair they could send back. The best package a team would accept (and that's still Fair for them) is shown, and one click starts a new negotiation with that team.
  • Walk away: recommended when none of the above is Fair for you.

Their trading style comes from their completed trades on Trade Grades. The same offer always gets the same answer — there's no randomness.

Trade Coach: building a trade

You build the trade one step at a time, and Trade Coach reads every version for both teams.

How it works

You build the trade you want, one step at a time, and Trade Coach tells you how each version reads. It starts with one search box: type a player (yours or theirs), a position or a team. If you don't have one in mind, start from a goal instead: more points, your title or playoff odds, trading from your strength, turning depth into a star, adding depth, building for the future, getting younger without losing points, selling high, buying low, or buying a position before its seasonal rise. Each lists trades that fit it and aren't bad for you, read the same way as the trade step, good for you first. A goal suggestion must also have a practical benefit for the other team: projected lineup help, useful depth, or picks and youth that fit its plan. A team without a rebuilding plan is not automatically asked to give up two or more projected points a week, counting depth. Offers requiring roster cuts stay out of these suggestions until those cuts can be reviewed. Their side explains the benefit and trade-offs. These are screening rules, not predictions that a manager will accept; manual trade building stays available. Each team's cornerstones (its best player, or a contender's top three) are tagged as rarely traded. In Superflex they also skip trades that would leave a contender under two QBs worth starting: in 630,481 real trades, a contender with exactly two sold one in 1.0% of its trades, against 5.5% with three. Rebuilders and teams out of the race do this far more often, so those trades stay listed and say it leaves them short at QB. Sell high and Buy low also have to beat KTC value by 3% or more, on pieces going for 8% or more over or under similar players. One row per player, so you get a spread of ideas, and any of them opens as a trade you can change. A player on another team goes straight to building. It opens as a pop-up with one decision on each screen, and the bar across the top fills in as you go; Back undoes your last decision. One of yours asks what you want to do with him: Upgrade (send him, and any others you add, for one better player; teams with someone better than the best of them come first), Down-tier (a lesser player at his position plus more; teams with one come first) or Sell (picks and young players; teams that need his position come first). A position asks what you want to do there, Upgrade, Down-tier, Sell or Add depth (players worth 3,000 or less, and well under your weakest starter, who still score), then which of your players you're sending. For whatever you picked, the Coach recommends the best fits: every realistic target (upgrades: more value than the best you send, or a point a game more from a position; down-tiers: 40-92% of his value at his position; sales: picks and players 24 or younger worth 50-130% of him) read as your players for him with the same read as the build step. For the closest of the rest, it tries every piece you have for the one that gets them to yes and leaves you best off, because extra pieces count for less, so the piece that works is often much bigger than the gap. Deals good for you come first, then Toss-ups, then the rest. Among equals, 2-for-1s come before 1-for-1s (the most common trade, and usually much closer to even): contenders send two first and rebuilders get two first, because at an even price the side that got fewer pieces was ahead later only 37% of the time, though consolidating helped contenders win titles. Selling or down-tiering, adding a piece of your own goes last. Each fit shows the verdict for them, what they're thin at, and how many more points a game it scores than yours. Fits include each team's cornerstones, tagged as rarely traded. A contender's last starting QBs sit under Left out, one click from building the trade anyway. A team lists each one with its timeline and where it's thin or deep. Then the trade starts from that, and you add or take off pieces.

  • The read. Once both sides have something: the verdict for your team and for theirs, the price on trade value, whether their manager would likely say yes (the same model as Negotiate: their own read of the deal, their needs and their trading history), how much more you could ask for or how much more they'd want, your title and playoff odds before and after, and who changes in your starting lineup. All of it updates with every change.
  • Hints. Before the trade has two sides, pieces of yours that fill a need of theirs (and the closest to what's missing on value), or theirs that fill yours. After, at a no: the single pieces of yours that get them to yes; two pieces if no one piece leaves you ahead (never your starters, and never a piece that would do it alone); or failing that, the ones that get them closest, each with what they'd still want. At a yes: one more of their pieces you could ask for, or a piece of yours you could keep or swap for something cheaper, with the room left. It never builds the trade for you.
  • Then negotiate it (their manager answers round by round), open it in the builder, or copy the offer link.

Until October 27 the Coach suggested whole trades in a dozen lists (Best trades, Sell high, Rebuild and others). About a third of what they listed failed the manager model Negotiate uses, so a listed trade could be declined the moment you offered it; building it yourself with one read keeps the two from disagreeing.
